## Then decide how exact each barrier must be

Colored Gibbs sampling places a barrier between color steps so every update sees committed neighbors. That preserves the intended transition rule, but a device-wide barrier can cost more than the useful work at small problem sizes.

The paper studies two ways to use the idle time. **Multi-rate scheduling** holds cut-facing spins until fresh boundary values arrive while interior spins continue updating. The boundary transition remains exact when it eventually runs, although mixing can slow near the seam. **Bounded staleness** updates the boundary from a halo with limited age. It increases throughput but perturbs the conditional distribution, so its quality cost must be measured.

Both controls create an interior optimum. Relaxing synchronization first cuts sweep time faster than it lowers success probability. Past the optimum, quality loss dominates and time-to-solution rises again.

The graph determines the safe range. Sparse partitions expose a small boundary relative to the interior, so the penalty remains local. Dense graphs spread stale information through many neighbors and require more frequent synchronization. The schedule is therefore a workload decision, not a universal switch.
